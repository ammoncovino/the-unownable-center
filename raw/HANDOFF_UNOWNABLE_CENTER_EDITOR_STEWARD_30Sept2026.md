### 4.3 What the book argues, in its own words

The governing claim in the Preface: every durable system builds itself around a **center it did not create and cannot finally own**. Empires call it order, religions call it God, markets call it value, platforms call it engagement, AI labs call it intelligence. The center is unownable because it precedes, exceeds, and outlives the system. Crisis begins when an institution mistakes stewardship for possession and treats access to the center as property.

The method the Preface states: before deciding whether a system is good, bad, sacred, secular, true, false, conservative, progressive, ancient, or modern, examine how it behaves. What does it reward, hide, compress, call reality? What happens when the model stops matching the world? The Preface also says the book is not doctrine, deconversion, partisan argument, or prophecy, and that agreement is not the first goal.

The test cases: Scripture (the Lord's Prayer, Genesis, the golden calf, the prophets, the red letter sayings, Gethsemane, the crucifixion, Gog and Magog), constitutions, Rome, arena stories (The Hunger Games is named), Reddit, AI, and personal identity. The closing reflection names the unownable center as what a system depends on for legitimacy or survival but cannot rightfully possess: truth, God, reality, the commons, the future.
